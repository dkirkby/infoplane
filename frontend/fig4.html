Figure 4 — the gradients, and the two phases of SGD

This figure is not about information. For each of the six weight matrices it plots the norm of the mean minibatch gradient against the norm of their spread, on log-log axes, starting from the untrained network. The paper's claim is that the mean dominates early — SGD drifting downhill — and is later swamped by batch-to-batch noise, so the weights diffuse under the error constraint; that second phase is the mechanism it offers for compression. The quantity the paper defines cannot produce the figure the paper prints — the published curves open a hundredfold apart and this one has a ceiling near three — and the reduction that does reproduce it contains no minibatch noise at all. Both are here, behind the control; the notes below are the measurement.

Mean gradient and its spread, per weight matrix

Solid: ‖mean gradient‖. Dashed: the spread of the same matrix. Faint: the same curves before smoothing.

Gradient SNR

The ratio of the two curves above. Where it falls through 1, the mean gradient stops being larger than the noise around it — the paper marks one such transition at ~350 epochs for the whole network.

Six curves, not five. There is one per weight matrix, so a five-hidden-layer network has six: (input, L1), (L1, L2), (L2, L3), (L3, L4), (L4, L5) and (L5, output). The authors take the even entries of tf.trainable_variables(), which is the same set, because the weights of each layer are registered before its biases.

Why the median, not the mean, over seeds. These norms are log-distributed across initialisations — the backpropagated signal is a product of five random matrices, and at epoch 0 a 50-seed ensemble spans more than a decade — so an arithmetic mean sits near the largest few seeds and is not a typical network. The paper's own figure is a single run.

How these are measured. At each checkpoint epoch, the gradient of the loss on every minibatch of that epoch is recorded at the weights that minibatch was evaluated at — the gradient the optimiser is about to apply, read before it steps. The mean is ‖E[g]‖ and the spread is √(E‖g − E[g]‖²), which is the authors' sqrt(trace(cov)) written without building the covariance matrix. The authors read theirs after the step instead — their training loop runs the optimiser and then asks for the gradients on the same batch again — which was measured here and moves the SNR by under 30% at every epoch, in either optimiser.

The published spread cannot be a spread between minibatches. At epoch 1 the paper's six mean curves sit at 0.078–0.080 and its six dotted curves at 7.8 × 10⁻⁴ down to 3.5 × 10⁻⁴: the mean gradient is 100 to 220 times larger than the batch-to-batch spread. Run the figure's own network — 12-10-8-6-4-2 with the two-way softmax the authors use — and both of its curves come out with the right shape. Medians over 20 seeds: the mean is flat across the six matrices at 0.024, 0.022, 0.020, 0.016, 0.021, 0.019, where the published ones are flat to within 3%; the spread falls with depth, 0.077 → 0.033, a factor 2.3 against the published 2.2. What is wrong is the gap between the two families. Ours sit at a ratio of 0.33–0.49; the paper's at 100–220. That ratio is the thing to compare, because it is the one number here a seed cannot move: the gradient norm itself spans more than a decade across initialisations, and the published 0.078 is an ordinary draw from ours. No batching closes the ratio. Cutting the batches at random instead of contiguously lifts it from a median 0.29 to 1.30 over 50 seeds; the ceiling, what it would be if every bit of batch-to-batch disagreement lay along the mean direction and none of it anywhere else, has median 2.6 and never exceeds 10.7.

The ceiling is set by the labels, not by the network. At initialisation this network answers 0.4908 to 0.5070 for every one of the 4,096 inputs — a spread of 0.003 — so each sample's gradient is (p − y) times nearly the same vector, and the only thing that separates one sample from another is which label it carries. The mean of (p − y) is 0.062, its sample-to-sample spread is 0.496, and a batch of 512 averages that down to 0.496/√512 = 0.022. The ratio of the two is 2.8, and that is the whole answer: no architecture, initialisation, optimiser or learning rate moves it, because none of them changes the labels. To reach 100 at this label balance you would need 650,000 samples in a batch, 158 times the entire dataset.

What does reproduce it: the spread between steps. Switch the control above to optimiser steps and the dashed curves are reduced over a different collection — the whole training set's gradient, re-evaluated at each of the weight states the epoch passes through. The data is held fixed, so there is no minibatch noise in it at all; what is left is how far the gradient moves because the weights moved. It lands on the published curve:

It goes on matching: the six ratios peak at 77–432, where the published curves open 100–220 apart, and the spread climbs from 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ to 7.3 × 10⁻², a factor of 560 where the published curve climbs about a thousand. The transition is late rather than absent — the crossings land at epochs 797 to 1,532 against the paper's single line at ~350, and (L5, output) never falls through 1 at all. The minibatch spread does none of this — it starts above the mean and stays there. What this does not establish is that it is what the authors ran: their released training loop feeds each batch's own rows to tf.gradients, and the one-line change that would produce this instead — feeding the whole training set — is not in the file we have. It is the only reduction tried here that lands in the published range, and it is reached through the control rather than made the default, because the minibatch spread is the quantity the paper defines.

And it would explain the transition. Adam's step is scale-free: it moves each weight by about the learning rate whatever the gradient's size. Early on the gradient is large and seven steps barely disturb it, so the step spread is tiny; late on the gradient has shrunk by two orders of magnitude while the steps have not, so an epoch's worth of them swings the gradient further than the gradient itself. A ratio that starts near 100 and falls through 1 follows from that alone, with no appeal to SGD noise — which is the mechanism §5.2 rests on.

The caption says normalized; the figure is not. Figure 4's caption reads "the values are normalized by the L2 norms of the weights for each layer, which significantly increase during the optimization". The authors' released code does not divide: calc_weights_norms builds flatted_list = [1] and returns 1 for every layer, with the real body commented out, and its call site is commented out too. Dividing is also what breaks the ordering. ‖W‖ for (L5, output) starts four times smaller than ‖W‖ for (input, L1) — three weights against a hundred and twenty — and then grows fourteenfold as that layer converges, so the ratio lifts its curve above all the others at the start and crushes it below them at the end. Undivided, the six curves start on top of each other and end ordered by depth, which is what the published figure shows. The toggle above puts the division back.

Why the paper's curves are smoother. Not because of more seeds. This page already takes a median over 50 networks where the paper's figure is a single run, and the noise that is left is mostly not averageable over seeds: every network steps through the same fixed contiguous minibatches in the same order, so they swing together. Measured directly — 16 seeds, the scatter of ‖E[g]‖ about a running geometric mean — one seed's curve jitters by 32% for (input, L1) and the 16-seed median still jitters by 23%, where noise independent across seeds would have left 8%. Two thirds of it is common to every network, and no number of seeds removes it. An average over a window of epochs does, and that is what the bold curve is. plot_gradients.py imports statsmodels and never uses it, which is where a lowess smoother would have gone.

And it sums over layers. Worth knowing before trusting any detail of that file against the figure: inside its loop over layers, plot_gradients.py plots y_mean = np.sum(means_all, axis=0), where means_all has just had the current layer appended — so the curve it draws for layer k is the running sum over layers 1…k, and the spread curve is the square root of a running sum of spreads. Partial sums are nested by construction, each strictly above the last. The published figure is not: its six mean curves lie on top of each other for the first hundred epochs and then separate with (input, L1) on top. So the figure was made by some other version of this file, and the released one cannot be used to settle what it plots.

What this run shows, against the paper. Three differences, none of which went away under anything tried here.

  • There is no drift phase at the start, and there cannot be one. At the untrained network the SNR is already 0.29–0.33 for every matrix — the mean gradient is smaller than its own batch-to-batch spread before a single step — where the paper's figure opens two orders of magnitude the other way. Nothing closes that: not SGD at 0.4 in place of Adam, not reading the gradient after the step as the authors do, not shuffling the batches, not the figure's own architecture. The ceiling above says why. This is the one difference from the paper that is not a matter of settings.
  • The transition is per-layer, not global. The three matrices nearest the input never rise above SNR 1 at all; the three deepest peak at epochs 115, 127 and 130 and fall back through 1 at 142, 240 and 832. The paper marks one transition for the whole network, at ~350 epochs.
  • Only the shallow matrices' spread rises. It grows 27× for (input, L1), 17× for (L1, L2) and 7× for (L2, L3), all peaking around epoch 1,500. The three deepest barely move — 1.7×, 1.1×, 1.0× — peak inside the first 70 epochs, and then fall by one to two orders of magnitude as the network converges. In the paper all six rise together, by about three orders of magnitude.

The earlier peak is consistent with the rest of this replication — the information plane turns at ~85 epochs rather than ~350 — and follows from the optimiser the authors' code uses, Adam at 4e-4, rather than the plain SGD the paper's text describes and whose gradient noise this argument is about. The missing drift phase is not explained by that, and it is not a measurement artifact: these norms were recomputed from per-sample gradients, one sample at a time, and agree with the training loop's numbers to six figures. One contributor to the size of our spread is identifiable — the authors' minibatches are contiguous slices of a file ordered by input pattern, so they differ systematically in label balance (p(y=1) = 0.22 … 0.65) and inflate it about fourfold against shuffled batches. That is a factor of four against a factor of three hundred, and the notes above are where the rest of it goes.
