Figure 5 — the plane at six depths

Six networks, one to six hidden layers, each trained on the same 80% of the patterns and averaged over 50 seeds. The widths grow from the input end — 10, then 10-7, then 10-7-5 — so the five-layer panel is the architecture every other page here uses, and the sixth is that one with a second 4-wide layer inserted. The paper's question is what the hidden layers are for; its answer is that depth buys compression cheaply.

What each depth reaches

The six-layer panel is the one measurement here with a problem. Its architecture is 10-7-5-4-4-3 — the authors' own, with two consecutive 4-wide layers — and between exactly that pair the estimator inverts the data processing inequality in 4 of 50 seeds, 186 times out of 77,100, by up to 0.39 bits. I(X;T) cannot rise with depth, so those are estimator error, not measurement: binning layer i+1 is not a coarsening of binning layer i, and when the map between two layers of the same width is close to invertible the 30-bin quantisation swamps the little information the layer actually loses. It is the only pair of equal consecutive widths in the sweep and the only violation above a hairline — the others are 1 to 23 inversions of under 0.01 bits between two layers sitting at 11.99 bits, which is the usual saturated pair. Nothing here has been tuned to hide it; the table above carries the counts.

Which architectures these are, and why not the caption's. The caption says "the width of the hidden layers start with 12, and each additional layer has 2 fewer neurons", and §3.6 adds "the first hidden layer (of width 12)". The authors' code says otherwise, and it says it about this exact figure: plots/plot_figures.py builds a 2×3 grid titled "One hidden layer" … "Six hidden layers" and feeds it ff3_5, ff3_4, ff3_3, ff3_2, ff3_1 and a run named layerSizes=10,7,5,4,4,3. The ff3_N suffix is the net_type argument, and select_network_arch maps 5, 4, 3, 2, 1 to [10], [10,7], [10,7,5], [10,7,5,4], [10,7,5,4,3] — one to five hidden layers, in panel order, with the sixth spelled out in the path. The widths grow from the input end, the decrements are 3, 2, 1, 1 rather than 2, and the 12 is the input, counted as a hidden layer exactly as §4.1 does when it writes the network as "12-10-7-5-4-3-2". This project follows the code.

So the five-layer panel is the main run. 10-7-5-4-3 is the architecture behind Figures 2, 3 and 4 here, trained the same way on the same 80% head split, which makes that panel a check on the whole pipeline rather than a new measurement: it should reproduce Figure 2's ensemble-mean paths, and it does. The caption's family would have made it 12-10-8-6-4 and comparable to nothing.

The paper's Figure 5 is plotted in nats, not bits. Its axes run to 9 and to 0.7 where every other information-plane figure in the paper runs to 12 and to 1. Those are the same two bounds in natural logarithms: H(X) = 12 bits = 8.32 nats, H(Y) = 1 bit = 0.693 nats, which is exactly where its curves top out. Nothing about the experiment changed between figures — only the log base in whatever drew it. Read against this page, divide the paper's numbers by ln 2.

The output layer is counted as a layer. The caption's "final layer with 2 neurons is shown in all panels" is the readout — the authors use a two-way softmax where we use one sigmoid unit, the same model written differently — and it is measured here as the deepest series in every panel, as it is on every other page.
